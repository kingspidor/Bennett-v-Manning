## I. FEDERAL CLAIMS — 42 U.S.C. § 1983

### A. The State Action Problem and the Conspiracy Exception

Private attorneys are not ordinarily state actors. But the Supreme Court carved a critical exception in **Dennis v. Sparks, 449 U.S. 24 (1980)** (unanimous decision):

> "Private persons, jointly engaged with state officials in a challenged action, are acting 'under color' of law for purposes of § 1983 actions."

The Court held that private parties who conspire to use judicial process to deprive someone of constitutional rights are acting under color of state law — even if the judge involved has immunity. The conspiracy itself converts private conduct into state action.

The Eighth Circuit extended this directly to attorneys in **DuBose v. Kelly, 187 F.3d 999 (8th Cir. 1999)**, holding that private attorneys who conspired with the court to deprive a client of due process rights could be held liable under § 1983.

   - Effect: Created a closed evidentiary loop — evidence can't enter through DVPO (she says not relevant), civil claims can't proceed (she says DVPO resolved them)

3. **Spoliation and misrepresentation to the court:**

4. **Using court process to achieve an extrinsic end:**

**The constitutional right at stake:** Parental rights are a fundamental liberty interest protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. *Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57 (2000); *Santosky v. Kramer*, 455 U.S. 745 (1982). Deprivation of parental rights through fraud on the court is actionable under § 1983.

### D. § 1988 Fee-Shifting — The Attorney Magnet

Under 42 U.S.C. § 1988(b), a prevailing plaintiff in a § 1983 action recovers reasonable attorney fees. This is the hook that attracts contingency-fee attorneys. The calculation:

- Complex civil rights case lasting 2-3 years
- Reasonable hourly rate × hours × multiplier for quality/result
- Fee awards in parental rights § 1983 cases routinely reach six figures
- This is ON TOP of compensatory and punitive damages against defendants

### E. Statute of Limitations

## II. WASHINGTON STATE CLAIMS

### A. Abuse of Process

Washington recognizes abuse of process as an exception to the litigation privilege. **Mason v. Mason, 19 Wn. App. 2d 803 (2021)** (Division II) held:

> "Litigation privilege does not apply, and an attorney can be liable for abuse of process where the attorney was alleged to have intentionally employed legal process for an inappropriate and extrinsic end."

**Elements (Washington):**
1. Improper use of process in a judicial proceeding
2. Primary motive to accomplish an illegitimate end
3. Damages

**Application to Gass:**
- Used summary judgment process to seal a fraud on the court (Manning's contradictory declarations)
- Used collateral estoppel argument knowing it was based on a misrepresentation of what the DVPO court adjudicated
- Used the closed evidentiary loop to permanently sever Bennett's parental relationship — an end "without the regular purview" of summary judgment

**Note on Division conflict:** Division I in *Young v. Rayan*, No. 84426-1-I (2023) expressed skepticism about *Mason*'s approach. This creates a division of authority that the Washington Supreme Court may eventually resolve. But *Mason* remains good law in Division II, and the abuse of process exception to litigation privilege is recognized in the majority of states.

### B. Fraud / Civil Conspiracy

Washington litigation privilege has limits when an attorney participates in a client's underlying fraud, rather than merely advocating within the litigation.

**Gass's conduct exceeds advocacy:**
- She was notified of document fraud on 4/9/2024 and took no remedial measures
- She filed contradictory positions in two courts within 7 days — this isn't aggressive advocacy, it's misrepresentation of the record
- She admitted spoliation in email and denied it in court within 48 hours

**Civil conspiracy elements (Washington):**
1. Two or more persons
2. Agreement to accomplish an unlawful purpose, or a lawful purpose by unlawful means
3. An overt act in furtherance
4. Damages

Manning (Melvina) and Gass (and separately, Manning and Demaray) meet these elements. The overt acts are documented in writing.

**John Manning's role in the conspiracy:** John Manning prepared a declaration that was submitted to the court in support of Melvina Manning's case. Bennett's analysis identified it as fabricated — formatted as a text chat transcript (Yahoo Messenger screen names), not the audio call transcription it purported to be, with signs of copy-paste editing, formatting inconsistencies, and origination from John Manning's personal email days after the alleged conversation. If John Manning knowingly fabricated or materially altered evidence that was submitted to the court, he is a co-conspirator in the fraud — not a mere witness. His direct 50% ownership of Fall City Roadhouse ($1.3–1.55M) makes him a high-value defendant independent of Melvina's community property exposure.

### C. Fraud in the Inducement (Demaray)

**Elements (Washington):**
1. Misrepresentation of existing fact (or promise with no intent to perform)
2. Materiality
3. Speaker's knowledge of falsity
4. Intent to induce reliance
5. Justifiable reliance
6. Damages

The Communication Agreement was a promise Demaray knew could not be performed — it required a judge's signature he never obtained, contained a withdrawal window he knew was impossible from prison, and was never submitted to the court. Bennett relied on it in relinquishing parental rights. The damages are the loss of those rights.

### D. Legal Malpractice (Demaray only — if attorney-client relationship existed)

If Demaray represented Bennett in the relinquishment (even nominally), Bennett may have a malpractice claim. An attorney who drafts an agreement designed to be unenforceable while advising his client to relinquish fundamental rights has breached the standard of care.
